\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Assigning two colours to the edges of a drawing separates its crossings
into monochromatic and bichromatic pairs. We count only the former, as
if the two colour classes were drawn on separate layers with the same
vertex positions. The resulting optimization has two versions: one may
choose both the drawing and its edge colours, or one may keep the drawing
fixed and choose only the colours. This paper concerns the latter version
for complete geometric graphs.

Let $P=(p_0,\ldots,p_{N-1})$ be a labelled point set in the plane. Its
complete geometric graph contains the straight segment $p_ip_j$ for every
$i<j$. How hard is it to find an edge two-colouring with the fewest
monochromatic crossing pairs? This was Question~4 at the Crossing Number
Workshop in 2019 \cite[Section~1.4]{CRNW2019}, and it appears as the first
open problem in Section~5 of Aichholzer et al.\ \cite{Aichholzer2019}.
It was subsequently listed as Vogtenhuber's Problem~2 in the report of
Dagstuhl Seminar~21293 \cite[Section~4.12]{Dagstuhl2021} and as Problem~11
at Geometric Graphs Week~2022 \cite{GGWeek2022}. We prove that the threshold
decision problem is NP-complete, including when the given points have
integer coordinates.

\begin{definition}\label{def:problem}
The input to \emph{complete geometric two-colour crossing minimization}
is a list of pairwise distinct points $P\in(\Q^2)^N$ with no three
collinear, together with an integer $k$ in the decision version.
Coordinates and $k$ are encoded in binary. Write
\[
 E(P)=\{ij:0\leq i<j<N\},\qquad
 M(P,\chi)=\sum_{\{e,f\}\subseteq E(P)}
       \1[e\text{ and }f\text{ cross properly}]\,\1[\chi(e)=\chi(f)]
\]
for $\chi:E(P)\to\{0,1\}$, and put
$\OPT(P)=\min_\chi M(P,\chi)$.
The decision question is whether $\OPT(P)\leq k$.
\end{definition}

A proper crossing is an intersection of two open segments with four
distinct endpoints. Several crossing pairs may have the same geometric
intersection point; each pair is counted separately. The colouring is
unrestricted: it is not a proper edge colouring in the graph-theoretic
sense. There are no weights or prescribed colours.

\subsection{Previous results}

For a fixed drawing on $P$, let $X(P)$ be its \emph{crossing graph}: its
vertices are the edges of the geometric graph, and two vertices are
adjacent precisely when the corresponding segments cross properly.
If $C(P)=|E(X(P))|$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossing-maxcut}
 \OPT(P)=C(P)-\MaxCut(X(P)).
\end{equation}
Every crossing is either monochromatic or belongs to the cut induced by
the two colours. This relation makes MaxCut a natural source of both
algorithms and hardness results.

Aichholzer, Mulzer, Schnider and Vogtenhuber \cite{Aichholzer2018} proved
that MaxCut is NP-complete on segment intersection graphs. Their reduction
does not require the segments to form the edges of a complete geometric
graph. Adding all missing edges between their endpoints introduces new
colour variables and crossing costs; the minimum added cost can depend on
the colours used by the original segments. Thus the general segment result
does not settle the complete-graph problem. The six-point example in
Appendix~\ref{app:completion} illustrates this dependence.

The role of completeness is also visible in convex position. Masuda
et al.\ \cite{Masuda1990} proved NP-hardness for assigning the edges of
an arbitrary graph to two pages with a fixed vertex order. This is
equivalent to two-colour crossing minimization on a convex drawing of
that graph. For a complete graph, however, the optimum is known exactly.
Two chords cross
exactly when their endpoints alternate, so two-colour crossing
minimization is equivalent to the two-page crossing-number problem for
$K_N$. \'{A}brego et al.\ \cite{Abrego2013} determined this value exactly;
we recall the formula below and use it for two convex parts of our
construction. In Appendix~B of the full version, Aichholzer et al.\
\cite{Aichholzer2019} also studied the complete bipartite drawing between two convex chains and
gave a two-colouring with asymptotic crossing ratio at most $1/3$.
A rectangular version of this drawing forms our comparison grid.
For the reduction we need a stability estimate: its optimal colourings
must remain close to one of two reference patterns in the presence of
additional crossing costs.

Previous work on coloured crossing numbers has established several
extremal bounds. Aichholzer et al.\
\cite{Aichholzer2019} obtained bounds on the minimum over all drawings of
$K_N$, using small configurations, integer programming and a duplication
construction. They also proved that every sufficiently large complete
geometric graph admits a two-colouring whose monochromatic crossing
ratio is bounded away from $1/2$. Fabila-Monroy \cite{FabilaMonroy2025}
extended this ratio result to dense geometric graphs and any fixed
number of colours. For random points in the unit square, Cabello et al.\
\cite{Cabello2024} analysed a colouring defined by edge slopes and obtained
an expected monochromatic crossing count equal to $9/25$ of the expected
total crossing count. More recently, Hahn, Klinz and Vogtenhuber
\cite{Hahn2025} generalized the doubling construction to several colours
and improved asymptotic upper bounds using heuristic searches for small
initial drawings.

Approximation also benefits from the crossing-graph formulation.
Complete geometric graphs have $\Theta(N^4)$ crossings, so $X(P)$ is
dense on its $\binom N2$ vertices. The dense-graph MaxCut approximation
scheme of Arora, Karger and Karpinski \cite{Arora1999} therefore applies,
as noted by Hahn et al.\ \cite{Hahn2025}. Through
\eqref{eq:crossing-maxcut}, it gives additive approximation for the
monochromatic objective on the scale of $N^4$. Such a guarantee does not
determine the exact optimum. The result below settles that exact
complexity question.

\subsection{Result and proof outline}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The decision problem in Definition~\ref{def:problem} is NP-complete under
deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions. Consequently, computing
$\OPT(P)$ exactly is NP-hard. The decision problem remains NP-complete
when all coordinates are integers encoded in binary.
\end{theorem}

We reduce from \textsc{MaxCut} on permutation graphs, with a permutation
representation supplied as part of the input. Its NP-completeness follows
from de Figueiredo et al.\ \cite{Figueiredo2023}: their construction
explicitly produces the two permutations. After relabelling the first
order, a source graph can be described by a permutation $\pi$ of $[n]$,
with an edge $ij$ when $i<j$ and $\pi(i)>\pi(j)$.
For a source graph $G$, set
\[
 g(G)=\min_{x:V(G)\to\{0,1\}}
          \sum_{uv\in E(G)}\1[x(u)=x(v)]
      =|E(G)|-\MaxCut(G).
\]
Thus a source threshold $k$ becomes the equivalent integer threshold
$g(G)\leq t$, where $t=|E(G)|-k$.

The reduction must keep every target edge available for either colour.
We arrange the points so that a large family of original--frame edges
forms a rectangular comparison grid. A quantitative stability theorem
shows that every optimal colouring, even after bounded perturbations,
lies close to one of two reference patterns. This provides a reference colouring for the
rest of the construction without prescribing colours in the input.

Additional points, called program points, then control the difference
between the two colour costs on selected edges. These selected edges
encode a blown-up permutation graph. Their interactions with the other
edges of the complete graph are handled in two ways. A convex separation
excludes crossings between the comparison grid and program edges that
do not meet a frame point. The remaining costs are either evaluated
explicitly or bounded by errors smaller than the amplified source signal.
In particular, the background contribution is computable without knowing
an optimal source colouring.

The resulting estimate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-sandwich}
 K_{\mathrm{base}}+\Delta g(G)-E_-
 \ \leq\ \OPT(P)\ \leq\
 K_{\mathrm{base}}+\Delta g(G)+E_+,
 \qquad 2^{18}(E_-+E_+)<\Delta.
\end{equation}
Here the baseline $K_{\mathrm{base}}$, the amplification $\Delta$, and
the nonnegative error bounds are all computable in polynomial time.
The lower bound applies to an unrestricted optimum of the complete
graph, and one complete colouring supplies the upper bound. Since $g(G)$
is an integer, the gap separates consecutive source values and gives a
many-one reduction. We also specify a deterministic rational-coordinate
construction and bound its bit complexity.

The construction and its final threshold equivalence have been formalized
in Lean. Section~\ref{sec:formalization} describes the formal statement,
its assumption of the known convex optimum, and the complexity arguments
proved separately in the text. The proof source and exact finite checks
are available at
\begin{center}
\small\url{https://github.com/Myyura/two-colour-crossing-complete-geometric-graphs}.
\end{center}

\subsection{Conventions and scales}

We use $N$ for the number of points of the final geometric instance.
The letter $N_g$ will denote the number of vertices of a comparison grid
when a distinction is needed. For nonnegative integers $v$, define
\[
 Z(v)=\frac14\left\lfloor\frac v2\right\rfloor
                  \left\lfloor\frac{v-1}{2}\right\rfloor
                  \left\lfloor\frac{v-2}{2}\right\rfloor
                  \left\lfloor\frac{v-3}{2}\right\rfloor
 \quad(v\geq3),\qquad Z(0)=Z(1)=Z(2)=0.
\]
The theorem of \'{A}brego et al.\ \cite[Theorem~7]{Abrego2013} says that
$Z(v)$ is the optimum for a convex complete geometric graph: a same-page
pair of edges in a two-page drawing crosses exactly when its endpoints
alternate in the linear order. Boundary edges do not affect this count.
We use this established value for two convex background components.

For a source with $n\geq1$, the parameters are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parameters}
\begin{gathered}
 q=2^{20}(n+1),\quad S=nq,\quad d=S+1,\quad H=2^{20000}d^{40},\\
 W=H^4,\quad B=H^5,\quad D_m=512SW,\quad o=2SW+D_m=514SW,\\
 T=2^{13}o^2,\qquad A_0=2^{13}dT,\qquad
 \Delta=q^2W^4.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
All exponents and constants are absolute. The large constants simplify
the error estimates; the construction is intended to establish polynomial
complexity rather than produce small instances.

\subsection{Membership in NP and integer coordinates}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:np}
The problem in Definition~\ref{def:problem} belongs to NP. Rational and
integer coordinate versions are polynomial-time equivalent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $L$ be the total input bit length. Normalize denominators to be
positive and let $D$ be the product of all coordinate denominators.
Its bit length is $O(L)$. Multiplying every coordinate by this same
positive integer yields integer coordinates of $O(L)$ bits and preserves
all orientation signs. Computing $D$ and the scaled coordinates takes
polynomial time by ordinary integer arithmetic.

Pairwise distinctness and absence of collinear triples can be checked
by examining point pairs and the determinants
\[
 \orient(a,b,c)=(b_x-a_x)(c_y-a_y)-(b_y-a_y)(c_x-a_x).
\]
Declare invalid inputs to be no-instances. A certificate consists of the
$\binom N2$ edge colours. For every unordered pair $ab,cd$ with four
distinct endpoints, a proper crossing is equivalent to
\[
 \orient(a,b,c)\orient(a,b,d)<0,
 \qquad \orient(c,d,a)\orient(c,d,b)<0.
\]
There are $O(N^4)$ such tests, each using a constant number of operations
on integers of polynomial bit length. Count those crossing pairs with
equal colours and compare the result with $k$. Negative thresholds are
immediate no-instances. This verifies a certificate in polynomial time.
The same positive scaling is a reduction from rational to integer inputs;
the converse inclusion is immediate.
\end{proof}
